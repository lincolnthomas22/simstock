How to play

Everything in SimStock is pretend. The companies and coins are parodies of real ones, named and tuned to act roughly like them, but every price is simulated and so is the money. Nothing here can cost you anything, so the worst that can happen is you learn something.

One second
is one trading day
One minute
is about three months
You start with
$1,000 in cash

The market runs whether you are watching or not. At the top left are Pause, 1×, 2× and 4×: stop the days going by while you think, or run them up to four times faster. The space bar stops and starts it too. Everything paid by the day moves with it, so running at 4× pays four days of wages a second, and pausing costs you nothing at all. Today's date, counted in years, quarters and trading days, sits in small print beside Your firm on the front page.

  1. 1

    Find your way around

    The bar across the top of every room is how you move about. Home takes you back to Mr. Sim, and the buttons on the right jump straight to any room, including Crypto and Memecoins beside the trading floor. The room you're in is the green one.

    • 1 Back to the home screen, where the portfolio and this guide live.
    • 2 The room you are in now.
  2. 2

    Open a brokerage account

    A brokerage account is what lets you buy shares. On the front page, press the gold Open an account button. You'll get three short cards explaining what a share is, then the account opens and you land on the trading floor with three companies and the index fund to choose from.

    • 1 Press here. You only do this once.
  3. 3

    Read the trading floor

    This is where you'll spend most of your time. Pick a company on the left, study it in the middle, and buy or sell on the right.

    • 1 The watchlist. Every company, grouped by the account tier that unlocks it. Click one to look at it. Greyed-out names are ones you can watch but not yet trade.
    • 2 Today's price and how much it moved since yesterday. Green is up, red is down.
    • 3 The chart. Switch between one month, three months and a year. Hover it to see the price on any day. The gold dashed line is what you paid, so anything above it is profit.
    • 4 The facts about the company, each with a plain-English note under it. The 52-week range bar above them shows where today's price sits between the year's lowest and highest. The price strip along the top of the screen shows the same range for every company, and flags any that hit a new high or low today.
    • 5 The order ticket, where you buy and sell.
    • 6 Your shares in this company, and whether you are up or down on them.
  4. 4

    Place your first order

    Orders fill instantly at the price on screen. On top of that the broker takes a commission of 0.25% of the trade, and never less than $1. You pay it going in and again coming out, so a share has to rise by about half a percent before a quick round trip is worth doing at all.

    • 1 Choose Buy or Sell.
    • 2 Type how many shares, or use the minus and plus buttons.
    • 3 Max fills in as many as your cash allows, or when selling, everything you own.
    • 4 The commission, what the broker charges to put the trade through. On a small order the $1 minimum is most of it, which is why buying one share at a time is an expensive habit.
    • 5 Press the button to place the order. If it is greyed out, the note underneath says why, usually not enough cash.

    Watch Cash afterwards. It already has the commission taken out, and spending every last dollar leaves you nothing to buy with when a price drops.

  5. 5

    Make money three ways

    There is no single right way to play, but money reaches you through three doors.

    • Selling for more than you paid. Your gain is only "on paper" until you sell. Sell, and it becomes cash you keep. That is the difference between the Gain or loss and Profit from sales lines.
    • Dividends. Some companies pay their shareholders cash every quarter, roughly once a minute. Hold the shares and it lands in your account by itself. The dividend yield stat tells you who pays.
    • Staff, minus their wages. Staff bring in client fees every trading day, and draw wages every day. Fees rise and fall with the market. Wages don't. In a good stretch they make you money, and in a bad one they cost you, so keep cash aside. If you can't cover the wages, someone gets let go.

    Watch the Staff, net figure at the top of the screen. Green means your desk pays for itself. Red means it's eating your cash.

  6. 6

    Try crypto and memecoins

    The Crypto and Memecoins rooms work just like the trading floor: a list on the left, the coin in the middle, the order ticket on the right. The same account tiers unlock them, and buying and selling earns the same XP. What changes is how they behave.

    • Coins are not companies. There are no profits behind them and no dividends. A coin is worth whatever the next buyer will pay, which is why the whole crypto market can rise or fall a tenth in a day on one piece of news.
    • You can buy a slice of a coin. A whole Bitcoyn costs tens of thousands of dollars, but you can buy 0.001 of one, or type in any amount. The minus and plus buttons move by about $100 at a time.
    • Tethur is a stablecoin. It is meant to stay at exactly $1, so it is somewhere to park cash, not something that grows. Now and then it slips for a day.
    • Memecoins cost a tiny fraction of a cent, so you buy them by the million. Some achievements are for simply holding a million, a billion or a trillion coins of one memecoin.
    • Some memecoins have already ascended. Doggecoin and the other big names swing wildly, but they are too famous to disappear. Newer coins that ascend can't be rugged either, but sooner or later the crowd moves on and they fade away, so watch for the warning.
    • The rest are about to ascend, or so their holders hope. On any day one might catch fire and multiply 20 to 400 times over a week or two, then join the ascended coins, and a new one launches in its place. Many never make it: the people who made them pull the plug and vanish with the money, and the coins are worth nothing. That is called being rugged.
    • 1 Already ascended: big, famous and still wild, but they won't be rugged.
    • 2 About to ascend: tiny, new, and either on their way up or on their way to nothing. A coin that is taking off right now says so.
    • 3 Buy by the million. Max works out exactly how many your cash covers.

    Keep memecoins to money you could lose entirely. The big wins are real, and so are the rug pulls.

  7. 7

    Check your portfolio

    The Portfolio room adds everything up: your net worth, how much of it is cash and how much is invested, every company and coin you hold, and a set of bars showing how your money is split. If one bar is much longer than the rest, most of your fortune is riding on one thing.

    At the top of the room is the chart that matters most, You against the market. The solid line is your net worth. The grey dashed line beside it is what the same money would have done sitting in the whole market, untouched. Your line goes green when you are ahead of it and red when you are behind. Beating that dashed line is harder than it looks, and it is the honest test of whether your trading is doing anything for you.

    • 1 Click any row to open that company or coin in the room that trades it.
  8. 8

    Grow the firm in Upgrades

    The Upgrades room has the two things that make your desk bigger.

    • Account tiers. Starter, Silver, Gold, Platinum, Diamond and Obsidian. Each one needs a level and a one-off fee, from $5,000 for Silver up to $10,000,000 for Obsidian. Every tier opens the same amount: three more companies, two more coins and two more memecoins, working up from a power company to AI chips, and from Bitcoyn to the wildest coins on the board. Anything you already own stays yours to sell, whatever your tier.
    • Staff. A Research Analyst costs $50 to hire, brings in $1 a day in fees and draws $0.45 a day in wages. Each hire costs a little more than the last, and better roles unlock with better accounts. The page tells you how clients are feeling, which is what decides the fees. Everyone you hire arrives with a name and a quirk, listed under their role; press a name to read theirs. The quirks are just for fun and don't change what anyone earns.

    Keep an eye on The firm at the bottom of the front page, too: your desk picks up a pile of research notes, a plant, a second screen and a framed first dollar as the firm grows.

    Your level is the bar at the top of the screen. You earn XP by trading, hiring and collecting dividends, and every level pays a cash bonus.

  9. 9

    Things worth knowing

    • Your progress saves by itself, in this browser. Another device, or a clearing of your browsing data, would otherwise start you over, so Settings at the top right has Save to a file: it puts your whole game in your downloads as a small file. Load a file on any browser picks it back up where you left off. Settings can also turn the sound effects and the music off or back on, turn the music down, and reset the game on purpose.
    • Prices move on their own every second, and every company reports earnings each quarter. Good news usually lifts a price and bad news drops it, but not always.
    • Booms and recessions are rare, about one every half hour of play at normal speed, and you get a notice when one starts and when it ends. While one is under way, a tag under the speed buttons says so. A recession lasts six months to a year of trading days: the market can lose a third of its value, shaky companies fail more often, and airlines, carmakers and banks fall hardest while utilities, groceries, medicine and gold hold up best. Prices usually start recovering before the recession is declared over. A boom lifts almost everything, cyclical companies most, and ends when interest rates go up. Crypto follows the stock market in both, only harder.
    • Spread your money out. Owning several companies means one bad earnings report cannot wipe you out. The index fund, VTMF, holds a slice of the whole market and is the calmest place to start.
    • Every company has a risk rating from 1 to 5, shown as a row of pips beside its ticker and spelled out on its page. Green pips (1 and 2) are steady companies that grow slowly. Gold (3) is in between. Red (4 and 5) swing hard, and can jump or fall by a tenth in a day. Riskier companies grow faster on average, which is the reward for putting up with the swings. Keep those positions small until you are comfortable.
    • Companies rated 3 or higher can fail completely, and the higher the rating, the likelier it is. Usually there is a warning first: bad news knocks the price down and the company says it may not be able to pay its debts. From then on it might collapse on any day, unless the price recovers. A company rated 5 can occasionally collapse overnight with no warning at all. When a company fails, the shares are worthless and the money does not come back. That is the price of the faster growth, and it is why nobody sensible puts everything into one wild company. A few weeks later a new company lists on the exchange and takes the empty place, with its own risk rating.

That's everything. The market is open whenever you are.
